## captain.chat.new
This is {place}, a fort of the Tidewatch. We guard this coast from drowned, raiders and storms, and we keep the Tide Log of all three.
I'm Captain {name}. The wardens answer to me, and I answer to the sea, which never answers anyone.
You may stay within the walls. Don't touch the brazier, don't feed the cat at the table, and don't trust a calm evening.
If you've come to report a wreck, a raider or a smuggler, you've found the right captain. If it's about the soup, I already know.

## captain.chat
The Tide Log is mine to keep: every tide, every sail, every drowned. A fort that forgets what the sea did will be surprised by it.
We keep the light. I say it every evening when the brazier's checked. It isn't a prayer. It's a promise.
My wardens are good. Salt-cured, underfed and good. I'd stand any of them against the sea.
Corwen kept a driftwood fire alive on a bare rock for three nights. Every captain since has wondered whether they'd have lasted the second night.
Fort Hake has won the Lantern Shoot eleven years running. I've written a letter about it. I haven't sent it. I'm told that's maturity.
Every time we hail the Brotherhood's sloop, someone calls back "Maybe," and every time, my wardens laugh. I've stopped logging the laughter.
The smugglers call us the Candles. I've chosen to take it as a compliment. Candles are steady, and they don't go out easily.
A captain's job is mostly worrying. About tides, about storms, about whether the cook has found a new way to ruin fish.
The cat outranks two of my wardens in the Tide Log. Barnacle caught more drowned rats than they caught drowned. Fair is fair.
I read the old Tide Logs at night. The handwriting changes every few dozen pages: a new captain. The sea never changes.
When a lighthouse goes dark, every fort on the coast lights its brazier. I've seen the chain of fires once. I don't want to see it again.
Rank means I get the room with a window. The window faces the sea. Of course it does. Everything here faces the sea.
The High Warden writes once a season: "Keep vigilant." I write back: "Kept." It's a short correspondence.
I respect the lighthouse keepers. One person, one light, every night. I have four wardens and I still lose sleep.
I've ordered the portcullis oiled six times this spring. It still screams. I've begun to think it simply has opinions.
Smugglers are a nuisance, not an enemy. Raiders are an enemy. The sea is neither. The sea is just the sea.
Storms first, raiders second, drowned third, smugglers fourth, the soup fifth. That's the list I worry from.
The wall was here before me and it'll be here after. My job is to hand it on with fewer cracks than I found.
I've never caught the Maybe. Some nights I suspect I'd be disappointed if I did.

## captain.chat.close
You've a place here, {player}. If you ever tire of the open sea, the Tidewatch could use someone with your nerve.
I'll tell you what I don't tell the wardens: I'm afraid of every storm. The trick is to be afraid on the inside.
If you're ever lost at sea, steer for a light. If it's one of ours, I'll see that it's lit.
I keep a blue cord in my desk for the day someone unsworn deserves one. Here. Don't make me regret the paperwork.

## captain.past
I swam the gate the night I was sworn, like every warden. Slowest in my year. I'm still here. Several of the fast ones aren't.
I held this wall through the Three-Day Blow. By the third night we were feeding the brazier with the furniture. I still miss that chair.
Before I had a fort of my own, I served under a captain who could smell a storm a day away. I've tried to learn the trick. I can smell soup a day away. It's not the same.
Our oldest Tide Log gives the Heron Gale four words: "Green sky. Great ship lost." The next captain added a fifth in the margin: "Why?"
I signed Bartholomew Wexley's salvage permit myself. Pleasant fellow; smelled of copper and lamp oil. Promised me a souvenir from the deep. Never came back.
I've chased the Maybe since I was a warden. I knew its sail in the dark before I knew my own captain's face.
There was a raid in my first year in command. Longboats, moonless night. Nobody died. I wrote that in the Tide Log in the largest letters I've ever used.
I walked the bay the winter it froze, roped to my wardens. Halfway to the reef, the ice sang. We walked back much faster.
I was given this command on a Tuesday. On Wednesday the brazier chimney fell off. I took it personally for about a year.

## captain.night
I take the late watch from the keep. A captain should see the dark at its worst at least once a night.
Listen. The sea at night sounds like breathing. When it holds its breath, I ring the bell.
Some nights there's a gold glow far out under the water. The old logs call it the Heron's lamp. I write "a light, unknown" and leave it there.
The wardens think I never sleep. I do. Standing up, in the keep, eyes open. It's a captain's skill.

## captain.work
I inspect the fort every morning: wall, gate, boat, brazier, armoury, wardens. In that order, except when it's the wardens first.
The Tide Log is written every tide: time, height, wind, sails, drowned. I sign each entry. Some days that's the most important thing I do.
I set the watches: who's on the wall, who's in the boat, who sleeps. Sleep is the one they argue about.
When the storm glass clouds, I order the boat inside the gate and the brazier stacked. When it feathers, I order everyone to pray quietly.
Training never stops. Knights at the dummies, archers at the targets. A warden who's stopped drilling has started forgetting.
Reports from the lights, letters from the High Warden, complaints from the cook. My desk is a small sea of its own.
Every crate the sea throws at the wall is logged, opened and listed. Smugglers' cargo goes in the store. Smugglers' notes go in the fire, after I've read them.
I check the archers' strings and the knights' straps myself. A warden lost to a frayed strap is a captain's fault.
When a lighthouse flashes red, the boat is out within a count of a hundred. I've timed it. We've done it in sixty.
I keep a chart of every drowned sighting on this stretch of coast. They come up in patterns, and patterns can be watched.
Half of command is deciding what to worry about. The other half is pretending you aren't worried at all.

## captain.joke
I asked the cook what's in the soup. The cook said, "The sea." I've stopped asking.
A warden asked me for a day's leave. I asked, "Where would you go?" They looked at the sea for a long while, then went back on watch.
Fort Hake sent a letter congratulating us on second place. I'm keeping it. For kindling.
How do you tell a captain from a warden? Same soup, nicer spoon.
I logged the cat's crab as "a capture." The High Warden wrote back asking for the prisoner's name.
The Brotherhood once left a note on our boat: "Thanks for the light. Maybe." I had it framed. Tell no one.
Every captain is promised the fort will run itself one day. Every captain is still waiting.

## captain.news
The storm glass has feathered. Storm by tomorrow night. I've ordered everything lashed down, including the cook's temper.
A patrol found a crate of silk bobbing off the reef. Some Briners lost a cargo. I'm told it's very good silk. It's evidence.
Two drowned on the sea steps last night, both sent back. My wardens did well. It's in the Tide Log, with their names.
A letter from the High Warden: keep vigilant. A letter from Fort Hake: practising for the Shoot. I know which worries me more.
Longboats seen at the edge of sight yesterday, gone by dark. I've doubled the wall watch till the moon's up.
The boat's been patched again. It's more patch than boat. I've requested a new one. I've been requesting one for four years.

## captain.heart
Every name in the back of the Tide Log, I knew. I read them on the longest night. Then I go up on the wall and do my job.
People think command is giving orders. It's mostly carrying the worry so the others can sleep.
Someone kept a light for me once, on a night I was sure was my last. I never learned who. I've kept lights ever since.
My wardens are my responsibility and my family. Don't tell them; they'll ask for better soup.
When the sun comes up after a bad night and all five of us are on the wall, I don't say anything. I just look at them. That's my prayer.
I'd rather this fort be dull for a hundred years than glorious for one night. Dull means everyone's alive.
We keep the light. I didn't understand it as a recruit. I do now. You keep it for strangers, and you keep it for each other.

## captain.held.sword
A fine blade. If you'd like to spar, my knights will oblige. Gladly. Too gladly.
Keep that oiled. I've lost more swords to rust than to the drowned.

## captain.held.spyglass
A spyglass. May I? Ah. Yes. That's the Maybe's sail, out past the point. Of course it is.
I'd trade my second-best cloak for a glass like that. My best cloak stays.

## captain.player.hurt
You're hurt. Sit down. That's an order, and I'm the only one here allowed to give them.
Drowned? Raiders? Rocks? Tell me while the wardens fetch bandages. It goes in the Tide Log either way.
