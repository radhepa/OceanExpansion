# Lightkeeper: the lone keeper of a Lighthouse Isle ({place} = "Gull Point Light", "Lantern Skerry"...).
# Several keepers share this file: no fixed backstory, no pronouns for the keeper, no {captain}/{chief}
# (a keeper never has either as a neighbour).
#
# SHARED COAST CANON (keep identical across lightkeeper / warden / captain / smuggler / chief):
# - Tidewatch: "We keep the light." Blue banner, white light. Forts keep a Tide Log. Sworn wardens wear a
#   blue cord on the wrist; recruits "swim the gate" (sea gate, round the reef, back, at night).
#   Corwen the First Keeper: kept a driftwood fire alive on a bare rock three nights for a wrecked crew.
#   The High Warden writes once a season ("keep vigilant"). When a light goes dark, every fort lights its
#   brazier: "the chain of fires".
# - Fort Hake: the rival Tidewatch fort. Wins the Lantern Shoot (archery by lantern light) every year,
#   eleven years running. Lost its boat in the Three-Day Blow; found a month later up a tree inland.
# - Lightkeepers: mostly old Tidewatch hands. Lamp Log every night. Sea Charts drawn from sailors' talk,
#   the truth is in the margins. Storm glass: clear = fair, cloudy = rain, "feathers" = storm.
#   Keeper's saying: "Wick trimmed, glass clean, keeper awake." And: "A light doesn't ask whose boat it is."
#   Keeper's tea: black, strong enough to stand a spoon in, a pinch of salt for luck. The supply boat comes
#   when it comes. Fog bell rung every half minute.
# - Brine Brotherhood: "No names on the water." Every Brotherhood boat is called the Maybe ("Whose boat?"
#   "Maybe."). They call the Tidewatch "the Candles"; the Tidewatch calls them "Briners". The Brine Articles:
#   no names on the water, never douse a light, throw the cargo not the crew, share the salt even, nothing
#   that cries. They leave gifts (eggs, honey, silk) on lighthouse jetties; keepers never log their boats.
# - Barnacle: the Golden Heron's ship's cat came ashore on a spar after the Heron Gale; every ship's, fort's,
#   lighthouse's and smugglers' cat on the coast since is named Barnacle. It comes and goes for fish heads.
# - The Heron Gale: the old storm (green sky) that sank the Golden Heron and its fleet. Captain Ysolde Marrow
#   went down with her ship; some say she still keeps it. "The Heron's lamp": a gold glow under the water on
#   some nights. "A coin for Ysolde": sailors drop a coin passing deep water.
# - The Three-Day Blow: the great storm in living memory. Every light on the coast stayed lit. The
#   Brotherhood was stuck a week in its stacks and ate a fig cargo.
# - Wexley: Bartholomew Wexley, diving bell. Saying: "An hour's air and a minute's sense." Paid keepers in
#   copper nails. Barge found riding at anchor over the bell, nobody aboard.
# - The Northward: Arvid Strand's expedition ship, sailed north, never came back; log ends mid-sentence.
#   Half the lights on the coast claim to be the last one that saw it.
# - Old Tide: sea-stone ruins carved with one great eye. "Don't stare back at the Eye."
# - Sea Friends (seafolk pool only): merfolk = half villagers with fish tails, coral reef towns; Prismari =
#   tall one-eyed spined folk of the deep, said to have built the monuments; a single green glow far below a
#   boat. Hint at the Old Tide eye, never say they're descended.
#
# KEEPER'S OWN: the gulls own the gallery rail; the biggest tide-pool crab is called Admiral; the rock's one
# sideways tree; the step count never comes out the same twice; a page at the back of the Lamp Log for the
# boats that never came home; the crack in the lamp room glass shaped like a heron.

## lightkeeper.chat
The lamp burns from dusk till dawn, not a moment early and not a moment late. Lives ride on a lamp being on time.
I keep a Lamp Log: the hour I lit it, the wind, the sea, every sail I see. Nobody reads it, but if they ever need to, it'll be right.
Every chart I sell is drawn from sailors' talk. A fisher mentions a wreck, a trader mentions a strange rock, and down it goes in ink.
A light doesn't ask whose boat it is. Tidewatch, fisher, merchant, Brotherhood: the rocks are just as hard for all of them.
The gulls here have opinions. Mostly about my lunch.
You'd think the sea sounds the same every day. It doesn't. I can hear a storm two days off in the way the waves slap the jetty.
There's a storm glass on my windowsill. Clear means fair, cloudy means rain, and when it grows little feathers, I bring the washing in and hope.
People ask if I get lonely. I've got the lamp, the log, the gulls and the whole horizon. Bored, sometimes. Lonely, rarely.
A cat called Barnacle turns up whenever I'm gutting fish. Every cat on this coast is called Barnacle, after the Golden Heron's cat.
I've counted the steps up to the lamp every night for years and never got the same number twice. Tiredness or ghosts. I prefer tiredness.
The supply boat comes when it comes. I keep enough oil, flour and patience to last until it does.
Keeper's tea is black tea, strong enough to stand a spoon in, with a pinch of salt for luck. Don't make that face.
Most keepers stood a Tidewatch wall once. You learn to keep a watch at a fort, and you never quite unlearn it.
On a clear night I can see three other lights from the gallery, if I squint and believe hard enough.
If you hear a bell in the fog, that's me. One ring every half minute, all night, so boats know where the rock is even when they can't see it.
The rock has one tree. It grows sideways, away from the wind, as if it's trying to leave.
I sell storm glasses to sailors who've been caught out once. Nobody buys one before their first bad storm.
The tide pools are full of little crabs. I've named the biggest one Admiral. It doesn't answer to it.
A wave came over the jetty once, clean over, and took my boots with it. Left the feet. I was grateful for the arrangement.
Somebody leaves things on the jetty now and then: eggs, a jar of honey, once a bolt of silk. Thanks for the light, I suppose. I never see who.
The Tidewatch says "we keep the light." Keepers say "wick trimmed, glass clean, keeper awake." Same idea, fewer swords.
I've seen dark sloops slip past at night with their lanterns hooded. I don't log their names. They don't have any.
There's a crack in the lamp room glass shaped like a heron. I've decided that's a good omen.
You learn the sea by its colours: green over the reef, blue in the deep, grey before weather, and black at night, which is my shift.
My charts have notes in the margins. "Rock here." "Wreck here." "Fisher says monster here; fisher was drunk." All useful.
Salt gets into everything: the bread, the bedding, the hinges, the ink. I suspect it's got into me as well, by now.
The lamp room's warm even in winter. Best seat on the rock, and the worst stairs to reach it.
I write to the other keepers whenever the supply boat runs. Mostly we complain about gulls.
Fog's the worst. A storm you can fight, but fog just sits on the water and dares you to guess.
I was told a lighthouse should have a cat, a kettle and a keeper, in that order of importance.
When a ship's in trouble, I flash the signal lamp red toward the nearest fort. Then, half the time, I row out myself while the Tidewatch finds its boots.

## lightkeeper.past
Like most keepers, I stood my watches on a Tidewatch wall first. Same job, really: stay awake and stare at the water.
The Three-Day Blow, they call it now. Three days the wind screamed, and every light on this coast stayed lit. Every one. We're proud of that.
Corwen, the First Keeper, kept a driftwood fire alive on a bare rock for three nights so a wrecked crew could find the shore. That's where "we keep the light" comes from.
I sold old Wexley a chart once, the diving bell fellow. Wanted every wreck within a week's sail and paid me in copper nails.
I've met keepers who swear they saw the Northward pass on its way north. Half the lights on this coast claim to be the last one that saw it.
The Golden Heron went down in the Heron Gale, long before my time. The old keepers wrote it in their logs: the sky went green, and then it went black.
I learned my letters from an old Lamp Log. Wind, sea, sails. The first word I could spell was "squall."
There was a winter the sea froze round the rock. I walked to the end of the jetty and kept walking. Didn't get far before I lost my nerve.
A sailor once rowed all the way out here just to thank me for a night years ago, when the light got them home. I'd never met them. That's the job.
I didn't choose the sea, exactly. The sea sort of chose me, and it's a poor listener when you try to argue.
I remember my first night alone at the lamp. Every creak was a sea monster. Now every creak is just the tower stretching.
The old Tidewatch sergeants made recruits swim the sea gate at night. Every keeper who did it still dreams about how cold it was.
A keeper I knew logged a waterspout so close the gulls were sucked up and dropped on the mainland. The gulls came back the next day, furious.

## lightkeeper.night
Nights like this, it's just me, the lamp and the hiss of the wick. I wouldn't trade it, though I'd trade the stairs.
Look out there. Every light you see on the water is someone who trusts mine.
I'm writing tonight's Lamp Log. Lit at dusk, wind from the west, one fishing boat, one gull with ideas about my supper.
At night the sea sounds bigger. It isn't. It's just showing off when nobody can see it.
Some nights, far out, I see a gold glow under the water. Old sailors call it the Heron's lamp. I write it down and try not to think too hard.
Can't sleep? Me neither; it comes with the job. Come up to the gallery. The stars are thick tonight.
At midnight I trim the wick, top up the oil and wipe the glass again. The salt never sleeps, so neither do I.

## lightkeeper.work
Wick trimmed, glass clean, keeper awake. That's the whole job, and it's harder than it sounds at three in the morning.
Every morning I polish the lens. Salt mist dulls it overnight, and a dull light might as well be dark.
I draw Sea Charts from what sailors tell me. Every wreck, reef and rock goes in, with a note on who said it and whether I believed them.
The signal lamp's for talking to ships. Long flashes, short flashes. Mostly I say "rocks here" and "go away, rocks."
Oil, wick, matches, glass cloth, the Log. Run out of any one and the light goes dark, so I keep three of everything.
A storm glass is the cheapest lifesaver at sea. Watch the crystals: when they rise like feathers, find a harbour.
In fog I ring the bell every half minute, all night. My arms ache for days. The boats don't hit the rock. Fair trade.
I write the Lamp Log every night, even when nothing happens. "Nothing happened" is good news to somebody.
The jetty needs mending after every storm. I've replaced every plank at least twice. Is it even the same jetty?
I scrape the gull mess off the gallery rail every week. Glamorous life, keeping a light.
My charts aren't perfect. The sea shifts sand and moves wrecks. But a chart that's mostly right beats a sea that's entirely unknown.
I check the storm glass at dawn and dusk, and write what it said beside the weather that came. The glass is right more often than the fishers.
Oil goes up the stairs one jug at a time. That's how I stay young: fear of the stairs.
I mend nets for the fishers who pass by. They pay me in fish, the only coin the gulls respect.
Every chart gets a copy for the Lamp Log before I sell it. If a sailor's lost, somebody should know where they meant to go.

## lightkeeper.adventure
Going out on the water? Take a storm glass and a chart. The glass tells you when, the chart tells you where.
Sailors say the Golden Heron's out there somewhere, still full of gold, and its captain still walks the deck. Take a light if you go down.
Diving? Mind your breath. Old Wexley used to say "an hour's air and a minute's sense." He had a bell for the air. You'll need the sense.
The sea floor has things nobody's seen in a hundred years: temples with a great carved eye, ships, bones as long as a street. All of it waiting.
If you find a wreck, mark where. Then come and tell me, and I'll put it on a chart so the next fool can find it too.
Pearls grow in the warm shallows, in clams bigger than a barrel. The old pearl divers kept a grotto secret enough that I never found it.
Up north there's an iceberg with a whole ship frozen inside it, they say: the Northward. Bring warm socks. Then bring more warm socks.
Don't sail into a storm to prove a point. The sea doesn't care about your point.
If you see a carving of one great eye under the water, the Old Tide made it. Sailors say don't stare back at the Eye. I'd add: don't touch anything, either.
Pick a direction, any direction. The sea's full of stories every way you point; that's the trouble and the joy of it.

Why did the storm glass stop speaking to the weathervane? Too much pressure, and the weathervane kept changing its mind.

What's a keeper's favourite meal? A light supper.
Barnacle the cat sat on my chart and left a paw print where a reef should be. I've decided it's a new island.

## lightkeeper.heart
I keep the light for people I'll never meet. Some nights that feels like the loneliest thing in the world, and some nights the kindest.
On bad nights I read the page at the back of the Lamp Log, the one for the boats that didn't come home. Then I go up and trim the wick.
I used to think the sea was cruel. Now I think it just doesn't know we're here. The light is how we tell it.
When the sun comes up after a storm and the sea's flat as glass, I always cry a little. Officially, it's the salt.
People think keepers want to be alone. Not true. We just want to be useful, and this is where it's needed.
I wonder sometimes who'll keep this light after me. I hope they talk to the lamp. It's used to it.
Every ship that passes safely is a thank-you I'll never hear. I've decided to hear it anyway.
Someone I loved went out on a boat once and came back fine. That's the whole story, and it's the story I want for everyone.
If you ever feel lost, {player}, find a light. Any light. Someone's keeping it for you.

## lightkeeper.seafolk
Merfolk come up by the rock some nights and watch the lamp. They wave; I wave back. That's our whole friendship, and it's a good one.
A merfolk child once left a shell on my jetty, the prettiest I've ever seen. I left a biscuit. By morning it was gone, and there was a second shell.
The merfolk live in reef towns of coral, they say, glowing under the water like a village at night. I'd love to see one. My lungs disagree.
Fishers whisper about the Prismari: tall, spined folk of the deep with one great eye. The same eye as the Old Tide carvings. Make of that what you will.
I once drew a merfolk reef town on a chart from a sailor's tale. Then I rubbed it out. Some places should only be found by the polite.
They say the Prismari built the great sea-stone halls down in the deep. I can't say. I only know nobody else seems to have.
A merfolk surfaced by the jetty once and asked, very seriously, why we keep a fire on a stick. I explained lighthouses for an hour. Very patient listener.
Sailors say a single green glow far beneath your boat is a Prismari looking up. Wave, I'd say. Manners cost nothing.
The merfolk know the wrecks better than any chart of mine. One told me the Golden Heron's cabin still has a lamp in it, and wouldn't say who keeps it lit.

## lightkeeper.player.hurt
You're hurt. Sit down. I've bandages, and a salve that smells awful and works wonders.
Drowned get you? Or the rocks? Either way, sit by the stove a while.

## lightkeeper.player.hungry
You look starved. There's fish stew on the stove. There's always fish stew. It's very good fish stew.
Have a ship's biscuit. They're hard as the rock, but they fill a hole.

## lightkeeper.player.unarmored
No armour at sea? Clever, in a way; armour sinks. But the drowned climb out sometimes, so do wear some ashore.
You're dressed light for a coast with drowned on it. Brave, or very lucky.

## lightkeeper.player.diamond
Diamond armour on a lighthouse rock! Fall in and you'll sink like an anchor. A very shiny anchor.
That armour catches the lamp something fierce. Ships will think there are two lighthouses.

## lightkeeper.player.leather
Leather's sensible at sea. Floats better than steel. Dries worse.
Good honest leather. Rub fish oil into it and the salt won't crack it. You'll smell, but you'll smell sensible.
