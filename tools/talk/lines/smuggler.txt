# Smuggler: the Brine Brotherhood hands of a hollow sea stack ({place} = "the Brine", "Cutpurse Stack",
# "the Hollow Tooth"... often starting with a lowercase "the", so never start a sentence with {place}).
# Rogues, likeable, never cruel. No pronouns for any smuggler or the chief. {chief} (the chief's first name)
# used sparingly.
#
# The chief falls back to these pools. The chief has its own greet, greet.stranger, greet.friend, chat.new,
# chat, chat.close, past, night, work, joke, news, heart, held.pearl, held.gold and held.map; EVERY OTHER
# POOL HERE IS ALSO SPOKEN BY THE CHIEF, so those must never mention "the chief" (use {chief}, which the
# chief skips).
#
# SHARED COAST CANON (same in all five files): Tidewatch ("We keep the light", Tide Log, blue cord, Fort Hake
# and its Lantern Shoot), lightkeepers (Lamp Log, Sea Charts with the truth in the margins, storm glass:
# cloudy = rain, feathers = storm), Barnacle (every cat on the coast is named for the Golden Heron's ship's
# cat), the Heron Gale and Captain Ysolde Marrow (the Heron's lamp, a coin for Ysolde), the Three-Day Blow,
# Wexley ("An hour's air and a minute's sense", copper nails), Arvid Strand's Northward, the Old Tide's one
# great eye ("don't stare back at the Eye"), merfolk reef towns and the one-eyed Prismari of the deep.
#
# BROTHERHOOD CANON:
# - "No names on the water." On the rock you have a name; out on the water you're nobody.
# - The Brine Articles: no names on the water; never douse a light; throw the cargo, not the crew; share the
#   salt even; nothing that cries (no people, no stolen pets, no poison).
# - Old Pepper: the founder, who ran a hold of pepper past three forts hidden under a boatload of mackerel,
#   and wrote the first Articles on the back of a fish crate.
# - Every Brotherhood boat is called the Maybe ("Whose boat?" "Maybe."). Ours broke up on the reef once; the
#   cargo was thrown, the crew all came home.
# - The Tidewatch are "the Candles": a nuisance, not an enemy; terrible soup. The keepers are honest; we
#   leave eggs, honey and silk on lighthouse jetties and they never log our boats.
# - Cant: "herring" = spices, "fish" = silk, "moons" = pearls, "a wet night" = a run.
# - Lantern signals: two shorts and a long = all clear; a long and a long = Candles about.
# - Sink-or-Swim: the dock dice game; the loser bails the sloop.
# - The Chief's Kettle: the hollow in the rock that whistles at night.
# - The Three-Day Blow: stuck in the stack a week, ate a whole fig cargo.
# - Wexley owed the Brotherhood for a crate of brandy (written off, lost at sea). The Northward bought three
#   barrels of the best brandy before it sailed, paid in full.
# - The chief keeps the ledger "No Names on the Water" and half a chart to the Golden Heron's grave.
# - The Brotherhood's Barnacle is a full member and has never paid its share.

## smuggler.chat
No names on the water. On the rock you can call me {name}. Out there, I'm just a voice in a dark boat.
The Brotherhood runs spices, silk and pearls. Pepper, cloves, cinnamon: things people want and the tax collectors want more.
The Tidewatch? A nuisance. Nice enough folk, terrible soup, ask a lot of questions. We call them the Candles.
Every Brotherhood boat is called the Maybe. The Candles hail us: "Whose boat?" And we call back: "Maybe." Never gets old.
Old Pepper founded the Brotherhood, the story goes. Ran a hold full of pepper past three forts under a boatload of mackerel. The forts smelled fish and waved.
We have rules, you know: the Brine Articles. No names on the water. Never douse a light. Throw the cargo, not the crew. Share the salt even. Nothing that cries.
Never douse a light. That's the oldest Article. Keepers don't take sides, and a dark lighthouse kills smugglers too.
We leave the lighthouse keepers a little something on their jetty now and then: eggs, honey, a bolt of silk. They never ask who. Good folk, keepers.
Lantern signals: two shorts and a long means all clear. A long and a long means the Candles are out. Three shorts means bring biscuits.
In the trade we say "herring" for spices, "fish" for silk and "moons" for pearls. A boat full of fish and moons is a very good night.
Our cat's called Barnacle, like every ship's cat on the coast, after the Golden Heron's. Ours is a full member of the Brotherhood. Hasn't paid its share once.
We play Sink-or-Swim on the dock. Dice game. Loser bails the sloop. I'm very, very good at bailing.
The sloop's faster than anything the Candles have, mostly because we actually look after it.
Dry cargo goes up high in the rock. Wet cargo goes in nets under a buoy only we know. Nobody wants wet pepper.
The cave echoes. You learn to whisper. Then you learn the cave whispers back, and you learn to stop listening.
The best customers are inland folk who've never tasted cinnamon. You should see their faces. Worth more than the coin.
We're not bad people. We just don't care to pay a tax on the colour of a shirt.
A good smuggler knows the tides, the rocks, the moon and the Candles' supper hour. The last is the most important.
The lookout at the top of the rock has the best view on the coast. You can see a Tidewatch sail a long way off. Very relaxing.
Silk gets everywhere. I've found silk threads in my boots, my bread and once in the cat.
The worst part of the job is the waiting. The second worst is the pepper sneezes. You can't sneeze on a night run.
We buy pearls from divers, traders, the odd fisher. All fair. All very quiet.
Rope bridges, rope ladders, rope everything. If heights scare you, a smugglers' rock is a bad hobby.
People think smuggling is all adventure. It's mostly carrying heavy boxes up wet stairs in the dark.
Fort Hake's never caught a Brotherhood boat. Too busy practising for that archery contest of theirs.
If the Candles ever find this place, the plan is simple: everyone grabs one crate and swims. We've practised. Badly.
There's a hollow up the rock where the wind whistles at night, like somebody humming over a pot. We call it the Chief's Kettle.
You learn to read the sea in this trade: calm, choppy, the Candles' boat coming round the point. That last one is weather too.
We don't run anything that cries, bleeds or poisons. Spice, cloth, pearls, the odd barrel of good brandy. Honest dishonesty.
Salt's the one thing we never smuggle. No profit in it. The whole sea's giving it away.
Ask {chief} about the ledger sometime. On second thought, don't. It's kept in a code that's unreadable on a bad day, even to its keeper.

## smuggler.past
In the Three-Day Blow we were stuck in the stack for a week and ate a whole cargo of figs. Worth a fortune. Tasted like one, too.
I've been chased by the Candles more times than I've had hot dinners. Caught once. Talked my way out. They're nicer than they look.
Old Wexley, the diving bell fellow, owed the Brotherhood for a crate of brandy. We've written it off. Lost at sea, like him.
The Northward bought three barrels of our best brandy before it sailed north. Paid in full, on the nail. Most honest customer we ever had, and look where it got them.
I once hid a whole bolt of silk inside a hollow mast while a Tidewatch knight leaned on it, chatting about the weather.
My first run, I was so nervous I rowed us in a perfect circle. Twice. The others still call it "the grand tour."
We ran pepper past a lighthouse once with the beam right on us. The keeper waved. We waved back. Nobody logged anything. Good keeper.
One night the fog was so thick we tied up to a Tidewatch boat by mistake. Very awkward. Very polite. Very quiet goodbye.
Before the Brotherhood I did honest work, mostly. It paid in calluses.
The night our old Maybe broke up on the reef, we threw the cargo and kept the crew, like the Articles say. Every one of us came home. That's when I knew this lot was family.
I learned the tides from an old Brotherhood hand who could tell the hour by the smell of the seaweed. Never wrong. Never washed, either.
I once found a coin on the beach with a heron stamped on it. Spent it on a pie. I've regretted that pie for years.

## smuggler.night
Night's when the real work happens. Quiet oars, hooded lanterns, and somebody always trying not to sneeze.
See the lighthouse out there? We steer by it. Never douse a light; it keeps us off the rocks as much as anyone.
On dark nights the drowned come up and the Candles stay in. The trick is meeting neither.
Listen. Hear that? Oars, out past the point. Probably fishers. Probably.
The night air in this cove smells of salt and pepper. Well. Mostly pepper.
Some nights there's a gold glow far out under the waves. Old hands say it's the Golden Heron's lamp, with Ysolde Marrow still aboard. We row around it.
I love the hour before dawn, when the sloop's back, the cargo's stowed and nobody got caught. Best feeling in the world.

## smuggler.work
Crates up, crates down, crates in, crates out. Smuggling is mostly a lot of crates.
Every crate gets a mark only the Brotherhood can read: a knot, a notch, a smudge of tar. The Candles have been trying to read them for years.
Wet nights we row the cargo out to the buoys in the dark. Dry nights we stack it high in the rock. Every night we count it twice.
Pepper sneezes are the enemy. You learn to sneeze into your elbow, silently, in a boat, in the dark. It's a skill.
We patch the sloop's sails ourselves. Patches on patches. If the Candles ever see it in daylight, they'll laugh themselves into the sea.
Watching for sails is half the job. The lookout up top can spot a Tidewatch boat before it spots us. Usually.
Silk has to stay dry, spices have to stay sealed and pearls have to stay counted. Pearls walk off if you don't count them.
The lantern up top talks to boats at sea. Two shorts and a long: all clear. A long and a long: Candles. Short, long, short: bring the cat in.
Everything goes in the chief's ledger, every crate and every coin. No names, of course. Just marks.
Hiding cargo is an art: under fish, inside loaves, in a hollow plank. Once we hid cinnamon in a Tidewatch officer's hat. Long story.
The trick to unloading is speed and silence. The trick to loading is not dropping crates on your foot. I'm still working on that one.
We sell to inland traders, river barges, and a baker who doesn't ask questions. The baker gets the best cinnamon. Look after your bakers.
Every coin's split fair. Share the salt even, says the Article. Nobody here's ever been short-changed. Not even the cat.
Scraping barnacles off the sloop's hull. Not glamorous. Necessary. Barnacles slow you down, and slow gets you caught.

## smuggler.adventure
Want treasure? Everyone says the Golden Heron's still out there with a hold full of gold. Mind the drowned on it; they're not the bargaining kind.
{chief} keeps half a chart to the Golden Heron, locked up tight. The other half is somewhere at the bottom of the sea.
If you go diving, mind the drowned. They're worse round the wrecks, and they don't take bribes. We've tried.
There's a pearl divers' grotto somewhere in the warm shallows, clams big as barrels. Find it, and we'll buy the pearls. Fair price. Mostly.
Old Tide ruins have a great eye carved on them. Don't stare back at it. Don't take anything off the altar. Or do, just don't tell me.
The Northward's up north, locked in ice, with our brandy still aboard. If you find it, raise a cup to Arvid Strand for us.
Smart adventurers carry two things: a light and a way out. Smart smugglers carry three: a light, a way out and a story.
Wexley's diving bell is out there somewhere. He used to say "an hour's air and a minute's sense." Take both.
Going far? Buy a chart from a lighthouse keeper. Best maps on the coast. We'd know; we've bought plenty under false names.
The deep water's where the strange things are: bones, ruins, places that glow. Everything worth finding is further than you want to swim.

## smuggler.heart
People think we're greedy. Most of us just wanted something more than a fishing boat and the same three streets forever.
The Brotherhood's family. We argue, we cheat at dice, we share everything. That's family.
Throw the cargo, not the crew. That Article's saved my life twice. I'd keep it even if it cost me everything.
Some nights I lie in my bunk listening to the sea in the cave and think: I'm lucky. Strange life, but lucky.
I've never hurt anybody, and I don't mean to start. The only thing I steal is the tax collector's sleep.
If I had a name on the water, I'd want it to be something kind. Since I don't, I just try to be kind.
My folks think I'm a fisher. I suppose I am. I catch silk.
The lighthouse keepers have never once logged our boats. I don't know why. I hope one day I can thank one properly.
The sea gives and the sea takes. I just stand in between and take a small cut.

## smuggler.seafolk
A merfolk trader once swapped me a handful of pearls for a jar of cinnamon. Best deal I ever made, and I think the merfolk thought the same.
Merfolk laugh at our sloop. I've seen them bob up by the hull, giggling. I don't know what's funny about it. Everything, apparently.
There's a rule in the Brotherhood: we don't cheat the merfolk. They know every rock and current, and an angry merfolk can sink you just by not mentioning a reef.
The Prismari, now, they're another matter. Tall, spined, one great eye, way down in the deep. Never seen one. Never want to. Well. Maybe once.
Old hands say the Prismari built the monuments, those sea-stone halls full of guardians. Same eye as on the Old Tide ruins. Funny, that.
A merfolk child once followed our sloop all night, curious, and kept quiet the whole run. Best lookout we ever had.
I hear the merfolk have whole towns of coral, lit up like a market at night. Imagine the trade. Imagine a tax collector trying to swim there.
If you ever see a single green light deep under your boat, it's polite to row away. A Prismari, some say. They don't like being stared at.
The merfolk know where all the wrecks are. Ask nicely and bring something sweet, and you might get a hint. Or a fish in the face. Depends on the merfolk.
