# Warden: the Tidewatch knights and archers of a sea fort ({place} = "Fort Saltmere", "Tidewatch Keep",
# "Seaward Hold"...). Knights and archers share this file: say "our archers" / "the knights", never "we archers".
# No pronouns for any warden or the captain. {captain} (the fort's captain, by first name) used sparingly.
#
# The captain falls back to these pools. The captain has its own greet, greet.stranger, greet.friend,
# chat.new, chat, chat.close, past, night, work, joke, news, heart, held.sword, held.spyglass and
# player.hurt; EVERY OTHER POOL HERE IS ALSO SPOKEN BY THE CAPTAIN, so those must never mention "the captain"
# in the third person (use {captain}, which the captain skips) or say "Warden {name}".

# SHARED COAST CANON (same in all five files):
# - Tidewatch: "We keep the light." Blue banner, white light. The Tide Log: every tide, every sail, every
#   drowned. Blue cord tied on the wrist when sworn ("you wear it till it rots, then tie on another").
#   "Swimming the gate": recruits swim out the sea gate, round the reef and back, at night.
#   Corwen the First Keeper: a driftwood fire on a bare rock, three nights, for a wrecked crew.
#   The High Warden writes once a season: "keep vigilant." When a light goes dark every fort lights its
#   brazier: "the chain of fires". A lighthouse flashes red at a fort for help; the boat goes out.
# - Fort Hake: rival fort; wins the Lantern Shoot (archery by lantern light) every year, eleven years running;
#   lost its boat in the Three-Day Blow, found a month later up a tree inland (we still send them acorns).
# - Barnacle: every fort cat is named Barnacle after the Golden Heron's ship's cat that came ashore on a spar.
#   The fort's Barnacle outranks some wardens in the Tide Log; its crab was logged as "a capture".
# - Brine Brotherhood ("Briners"; they call the Tidewatch "the Candles"). Every Brotherhood boat is the Maybe:
#   "Whose boat?" "Maybe." Silk crates wash up against the wall. Smugglers are a nuisance; raiders (longboats,
#   moonless nights) are the enemy; storms are worse than both.
# - Heron Gale: the oldest Tide Log gives it four words: "Green sky. Great ship lost." Captain Ysolde Marrow went
#   down with the Golden Heron; some say she still keeps it. The Heron's lamp: a gold glow under the water.
#   A coin for Ysolde when you pass deep water.
# - Three-Day Blow: the brazier burned three nights straight; every light on the coast held.
# - Wexley: Bartholomew Wexley got his salvage permit from a Tidewatch captain; his barge was found riding at
#   anchor over the bell, nobody aboard. "An hour's air and a minute's sense."
# - Northward: Arvid Strand, sailed north, never came back. Everyone on the coast claims they saw it pass.
# - Lightkeepers sell storm glasses (cloudy = rain, feathers = storm) and Sea Charts (truth in the margins).
# - Old Tide: sea-stone, one great carved eye, guardians. Sea Friends: merfolk (fish tails, coral reef towns),
#   Prismari (tall, spined, one great eye, the deep; said to have built the monuments).
#
# FORT LIFE: hardtack and salt pork; the soup is a duty; the boat is called the Steady (it isn't); the
# portcullis screams every spring; ballistae need restringing monthly; drill every morning.

## warden.chat
The Tide Log has every tide, every sail and every drowned we've seen since this fort was built. Some pages just say "fog."
We keep the light. That's the Tidewatch saying. A lighthouse, a brazier, a lantern on a pole: somebody keeps it lit.
Corwen, the First Keeper, kept a driftwood fire alive on a bare rock for three nights so a wrecked crew could find the shore. Every warden learns that before learning to hold a spear.
The drowned come up on dark nights, mostly near the reef. Every one goes in the Tide Log. The captain likes tallies.
The Brotherhood runs pepper and silk right under our noses. We catch about one boat in ten. The other nine wave.
There's a sloop we've chased for years. Every time we hail it and ask whose boat it is, a voice calls back "Maybe." Every Brotherhood boat is the Maybe, apparently.
The Briners call us "the Candles," because we keep lights. I can't decide if it's an insult. It sounds a bit like a compliment.
Every Tidewatch fort has a cat called Barnacle, after the ship's cat that floated ashore from the Golden Heron. Ours outranks me in the Tide Log.
Fort Hake wins the Lantern Shoot every year: archery by lantern light, every fort on the coast. I'm sure they cheat. I can't prove they cheat.
When I was sworn in, they tied a blue cord round my wrist. You wear it till it rots, then tie on another. Salt rots them fast.
The brazier on the keep is our loudest word. Lit means storm, or raiders, or come quick. It never means all's well. That's what quiet is for.
Raiders come in longboats, low and fast, on moonless nights. Our archers on the wall are the first to know and the last to sleep.
The sea gate's portcullis sticks every spring. Rust. We hang off the chains and swear at it until it moves.
The worst duty here is scraping barnacles off the boat. The second worst is the soup. Eating the soup counts as a duty.
Salt gets into armour, into hinges, into bowstrings. Into the soup, too, which is the only thing it improves.
We drill every morning: knights at the dummies, archers at the targets, everyone on the stairs until our knees file a complaint.
You can hear the whole sea from the wall at night. You learn its noises: wave, wave, seal, wave, drowned. That last one gets the bell.
Most lighthouse keepers stood these walls once. Now they get a whole tower to themselves and nobody shouting drill at dawn. Lucky.
When a storm comes in from the west, the whole fort hums. You feel it in your teeth.
A fisher once asked if we get bored out here. I pointed at the drowned crawling up the sea steps. Not bored. Damp.
The Tide Log is the only book here. The best bit is three pages a warden wrote about a gull that stole a boot.
Our boat's called the Steady. It isn't.
Barnacle caught a crab and brought it to the keep. It went in the Tide Log as "a capture."
We patrol the reef by boat twice a day. Mostly we find driftwood and very surprised fish.
If a light goes dark anywhere on the coast, every fort lights its brazier. A chain of fires. Then somebody rows out to find out why.
Our archers can hit a bobbing bottle at sixty paces. In calm water. On a good day. Never in front of Fort Hake.
The knights carry shields painted blue with the white light. Some paint little waves round the rim. The captain pretends not to notice.
Being a warden means standing still in the rain for hours and being glad of it. Strange job. Good job.
The pay is poor, the food is worse, and the view is the best in the world.
Never trust a calm sea in the evening. It's only catching its breath.
Fog's the worst. In fog the drowned can walk right up the steps. So can smugglers. So can the cook, with the soup.
Captain {captain} writes the Tide Log in a hand so neat you could frame it. Even the entries about the soup.

## warden.past
I swam the gate the night I was sworn. Every new warden does: out the sea gate, round the reef and back, in the dark. Coldest I've ever been.
In the Three-Day Blow the brazier burned three nights straight. We took turns feeding it in the rain. Every light on the coast held. Every one.
Fort Hake lost their boat in the Three-Day Blow. Found it a month later, up a tree inland. We still send them acorns.
I've been posted at more than one fort. The sea's different at each. The soup's the same.
There was a raid, years back. Longboats on a moonless night. Our archers saw the oars glint, and the bell rang before the first boat reached the reef.
I saw the Northward once, I swear it: a ship running north under full sail, late in the year. Everyone on the coast says that, though. Can't all be right.
The oldest Tide Log gives the Heron Gale four words: "Green sky. Great ship lost." Then three blank pages, as if nobody could bear to write more.
When I was a recruit, the old sergeant made us polish the brazier till we could see our faces in it. Then it rained. Then we did it again.
Old Wexley, the diving bell fellow, came through for a salvage permit. Later his barge was found riding at anchor over the bell, with nobody aboard.
My first drowned came up the sea steps on my first night. I dropped my spear; it dropped its trident. We stared at each other. Then I remembered I had a sword.
Before the Tidewatch I did a bit of everything, badly. Now I do one thing badly, in a tabard.
There was a winter the bay froze. We walked out to check the moorings, roped together like a string of sausages.

## warden.night
Night watch is the real work: eyes on the water, ears on the wind, and one hand on the bell rope.
See the lights out there? Fishing boats, a lighthouse, maybe a Brotherhood sloop with its lantern hooded. The sea's busier at night than people think.
The drowned come up when the moon's dark. Tonight's a dark one. Stay close to the lanterns.
I like the hour before dawn. Coldest, quietest, and you watch the whole sea turn grey like a page being written on.
Sometimes at night you hear singing out on the water. The captain says it's seals. I've never met a seal that could hold a tune.
Watch the lighthouse out there. While it's lit, all's well. If it goes dark, we light the brazier and somebody rows.
Night on the wall is when you think: about home, about the sea, about whether the soup's still on the fire. The soup is always on the fire.

## warden.work
Every tide goes in the Tide Log: the time, the height, the wind, every sail and every drowned. It's dull, right up until the day it isn't.
Morning drill: knights to the dummies, archers to the targets. Then everyone scrapes salt off everything.
We patrol the reef by boat twice a day, looking for drowned, raiders, wrecks and whatever else the sea coughs up.
The brazier's kept stacked and dry. When it's needed, there's no time to go looking for kindling.
We search fishing boats that come too close. Usually it's fish. Once it was a crate of pepper under the fish. That was a good day.
Checking the gate chains, oiling the portcullis, tarring the boat. A fort is mostly maintenance and shouting.
Our archers count every sail on the horizon. The knights check every boat that comes in. The captain checks us.
Before a storm we bring the boat inside the sea gate and lash down everything that moves. Then we lash down the cook.
Drowned with tridents are the dangerous ones. You learn to watch the throwing arm.
We keep the light. That means the brazier, the lanterns and each other. And the Tide Log, which keeps us.
A warden's kit: a helmet, a blade or a bow, a dry cloak, a wet cloak, and a sense of humour about the second one.
When a lighthouse flashes red, we row. Doesn't matter the weather. Doesn't matter the hour.
The ballistae on the wall need restringing every month. Salt and damp rot the cords, like everything else here.
We watch the sky as much as the sea. A green sky before a storm means a bad one. The old Tide Log says the Heron Gale came in green.
Guard duty sounds dull, and it is, right up to the moment it very much isn't.

## warden.adventure
Going out? Tell the fort which way first. If you don't come back, we'll know where to look.
The drowned are worse the deeper you go, and the ones dripping gold near the old wrecks are the worst of all.
If you find the Golden Heron, mind its captain. Ysolde Marrow went down with her ship, and sailors say she still keeps it.
Watch for Old Tide stonework under the water: sea-stone, dark stone and one great carved eye. The guardians there don't like visitors.
Buy a storm glass from a lighthouse keeper before you go. A sailor without one has a short career.
If you spot raiders, light anything you can and get back to a fort. That's not cowardice. That's the plan.
The Northward is out in the frozen north somewhere, locked in ice. Arvid Strand's crew is still aboard, the stories say. Not cheerful company, I'd guess.
Smugglers hide in hollow sea stacks. If you find one, tell the Tidewatch. Or don't, and tell me what their pepper smells like.
There's an old diving bell off the coast somewhere. Wexley's. Whatever went wrong on his last dive might still be down there.
The coast is long. A fort every so often, a light every so often, and between them, just you. Go carefully.

## warden.heart
Standing on a wall in the rain doesn't feel heroic. But every boat that gets home safe is because somebody did.
I miss home sometimes. Then I think: somebody's home is that light out there, and I help keep it lit.
The back of the Tide Log has names in it: wardens who didn't come home. We read them on the longest night of the year. It's quiet, after.
The people here are my family now. Loud, salty, badly fed family.
I'm not brave, you know. I'm scared every time the bell rings. I just go up the stairs anyway.
When the sun comes up after a bad night and you count heads and they're all there, it's the best feeling in the world.
Sometimes I envy the keepers. One light, one rock, all that quiet. Then I remember they cook for themselves.
We keep the light. I used to think it was about the brazier. It's about each other.
If I ever leave the Tidewatch, I'll still wake at the change of watch every night. You never really stop standing guard.

## warden.rumor.none

No word of anything out there. The fishers have gone quiet, which means either nothing's happening or everything is.

## warden.seafolk
Our archers swear they've seen merfolk out by the reef at dawn, tails flashing. They wave at the wall. We wave back. Officially.
Merfolk don't come near the fort much. Can't blame them. One whiff of our soup would scare anyone off.
A merfolk once surfaced by the sea gate and asked for whoever was in charge, very politely, to report drowned near their reef town. Into the Tide Log it went.
The Prismari, sailors call them: tall folk of the deep, spines down the back, one great eye. The old Tide Logs mention lights in deep water. Maybe it was them.
Some old hands say the Prismari built those sea-stone monuments in the deep, where the guardians swim. I'm not diving down to ask.
The merfolk know the sea better than any fort. When they say a storm's coming, we stack the brazier without arguing.
I once saw a carving of one great eye on an old sea-stone, and later heard a sailor describe a Prismari's face. Same eye. I didn't sleep well.
Merfolk children race our patrol boat sometimes. They always win. It's very bad for morale.
There's a rule in the Tidewatch: merfolk in trouble get the same help as anyone. The sea doesn't care who has legs.

## warden.held.pickaxe
